Atomic absorption: one element, one line

Every element absorbs light at a few sharp, characteristic wavelengths. Atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) turns a sample into a cloud of free atoms and measures how much of that light disappears. Start by picking an element below. Each dark line on the spectrum is a resonance line used in AAS.

Chapter 1

Basic concepts

AAS measures the absorption of light by free, ground-state atoms in the gas phase. Alan Walsh proposed the method in 1955, and it is still one of the most widely used techniques for determining metals.

  • Resonance lines. An atom absorbs a photon only when its energy matches a jump from the ground state to an excited electronic state (E = hc/λ). Free atoms have no vibrational or rotational levels, so their absorption lines are extremely narrow, about 0.002–0.005 nm.
  • Line source. A hollow cathode lamp (HCL) made of the analyte element emits exactly those wavelengths, so each element needs its own lamp.
  • Atomizer. A flame, graphite furnace, hydride generator or cold-vapour cell converts the sample into free atoms in the light path.
  • Measurement. The detector compares the incident power P0 with the transmitted power P. Absorbance follows the Beer–Lambert law: A = log(P₀/P) = εbc, so in the linear range A = K·c.

Chapter 2

Applications in materials chemistry and medicine

AAS is chosen when you need the total amount of one element in a complex sample at low cost and with high selectivity. The atomizer sets the working range: flame AAS for mg/L levels, graphite furnace for µg/L, and hydride or cold-vapour generation for volatile elements such as As, Se and Hg.

Chapter 3

How AAS differs from UV-Vis and emission spectroscopy

All three techniques measure light, but they see different species. UV-Vis detects molecules and complex ions in solution, so its signal depends on chemical form. AAS detects free ground-state atoms after the atomizer has destroyed every chemical bond, so it reports the total element. Emission (flame photometry, ICP-OES) detects light given off by thermally excited atoms and ions.

Chapter 4

Effect of temperature

In a flame at thermal equilibrium, the ratio of excited-state atoms N* to ground-state atoms N0 follows the Boltzmann distribution:

N*/N0 = (g*/g0) · exp(−ΔE/kT)g = statistical weights, ΔE = hc/λ, k = 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV K⁻¹

Even at 3000 K, fewer than 1 atom in 1000 is excited for most elements. AAS measures N0, which is essentially all the atoms, so a small temperature drift barely changes the absorbance. Emission measures N*, which changes exponentially with temperature. That makes emission much more sensitive to flame temperature.

Boltzmann calculator

Indirect effects of temperature on AAS

Temperature still matters a great deal in AAS, through the processes that create and remove free atoms:

  • Atomization efficiency. Hotter flames dissociate more refractory compounds. Al, Si, Ti and V form stable oxides and need the hotter nitrous oxide–acetylene flame (about 2900–3100 K) instead of air–acetylene (about 2400–2700 K).
  • Ionization. At higher temperature, elements with low ionization energy (Na, K, Cs, Ba) lose electrons, M → M⁺ + e⁻, which lowers N0 and the absorbance.
  • Chemical interferences. Higher temperatures break up compounds such as calcium phosphate, reducing chemical interference.
  • Line width. Doppler broadening grows with √T, which flattens and widens the absorption line (Chapter 6).
  • Flame stoichiometry and observation height. Temperature and the amount of oxide formed vary across the flame, so burner height and fuel/oxidant ratio are optimized for each element.

Graphite furnace: the temperature program

In graphite-furnace AAS (GFAAS) you control temperature directly. The program dries the sample, burns off the matrix during pyrolysis, then flashes the tube to atomize the analyte. Pyrolysis must be hot enough to remove the matrix but cool enough to keep the analyte. A matrix modifier such as Pd + Mg(NO₃)₂ stabilizes volatile analytes such as Pb and Cd so you can pyrolyse hotter.

Illustrative model: Pb in a salty (NaCl) matrix. Move the pyrolysis temperature and watch the analyte peak, the matrix background peak, and the pyrolysis curve.

Chapter 5

Types of interference

An interference is anything in the sample, other than the analyte, that changes the signal. It helps to sort them by their effect on the calibration line. Additive interferences add a constant signal and shift the line up. Multiplicative interferences change the slope.

How deuterium background correction works

The instrument alternates between the hollow cathode lamp and a deuterium continuum lamp. The narrow HCL line is absorbed by both atoms and background. The D₂ light passes through a monochromator bandpass of about 0.2 nm, which is roughly 40 times wider than the atomic line. The atoms absorb only a sliver of it, so the D₂ beam measures essentially the background alone. Subtracting the two gives the atomic absorbance.

Model assumes an atomic line 5 pm wide inside a 200 pm bandpass, so the D₂ beam sees about 2.5% of the atomic absorption.

Chapter 6

Line width and what controls it

The line width is the full width of the line profile at half its maximum height (FWHM, written Δλ½). An ideal transition would be infinitely sharp. Real atomic lines in an atomizer are about 0.002–0.005 nm (2–5 pm) wide because several processes spread the energy of the transition.

Why AAS needs a line source

A typical monochromator passes a band about 0.2 nm (200 pm) wide. If you used a continuum source, the 5 pm absorption line would remove only a tiny slice of that band and the measured absorbance would be very small. A hollow cathode lamp runs at low pressure and low temperature, so its emission line (about 0.5–1 pm) is narrower than the absorption line. Nearly all of its light can then be absorbed. Walsh's insight was exactly this.

Chapter 7

The standard addition method

When the sample matrix changes the slope of the response (a multiplicative interference), standards made in pure water give the wrong answer. In standard addition, known amounts of analyte are added to portions of the sample itself, so every measurement shares the same matrix. The response is extrapolated back to zero signal.

A = k · (CxVx + CsVs) / VtCx, Vx: sample; Cs, Vs: standard added; Vt: final (constant) volume

Plot A against the concentration of added standard in the final solution. The line has slope m = k and intercept b = k·CxVx/Vt. Extrapolate to A = 0. The magnitude of the x-intercept, b/m, is the analyte concentration in the measured solution. Correct it for dilution to get Cx.

|x-intercept| = b/m ; sx = (sy/|m|) · √( 1/n + ȳ² / (m² Σ(xi − x̄)²) )uncertainty of an extrapolated x-intercept
Requirements. The response must be linear over the whole addition range. The signal must be blank-corrected. Additive interferences must be removed first, for example by background correction. Standard addition corrects slope errors, not offsets.
